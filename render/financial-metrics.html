A five-minute health check before you value anything

Every model you run is only as honest as the business you put into it. That is why QuantaVal's financial metrics masterclass begins a step before valuation, with four ratios that each ask a different question: the P/E asks what you pay, ROIC asks how well capital is used, Net Debt/EBITDA asks how heavy the debt is, and the current ratio asks whether the bills can be paid. Run the four together, in the same sector context, and most bad ideas die in five minutes — before a DCF is even opened.

Ratios do not hand you a fair value. They do something blunter and more useful: they tell you whether a stock deserves the hours a real valuation takes.

The four formulas

P/E = Price ÷ EPS

ROIC = NOPAT ÷ Invested Capital

Net Debt/EBITDA = (Debt − Cash) ÷ EBITDA

Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities

Building Products clears all four and earns a deeper look — a DCF, not a buy decision. Its supporting numbers agree: DuPont decomposes its ROE into 7.5% net margin × 1.25 asset turnover × 1.6 leverage = 15.0%, and at a 55% retention rate that funds sustainable growth of about 8.25% a year without new capital. Retail Chain fails three of four, and the pattern matters more than any single number: a full-price multiple on a business earning below its cost of capital, carrying 4x leverage, with a liquidity deficit. No fair-value model fixes that combination; it only prices it more precisely.

When ratios mislead

  • Loss-makers. The P/E is meaningless on negative earnings; switch to growth-adjusted lenses like the PEG ratio or to cash measures.
  • One year, one cycle. Cyclicals look cheapest at peak earnings and sickest at the trough. Read several years, and check what reinvestment consumes on the CapEx / FCF calculator.
  • Financial firms. Banks and insurers do not use EBITDA or current ratios the way industrials do; judge them on ROE, book value and tangible equity instead.
  • Hidden leverage. Lease obligations behave like debt. A clean Net Debt/EBITDA that excludes them is a story, not a measurement — and a rising share count quietly re-prices every per-share ratio, which the dilution radar tracks.
  • Efficiency without economics. A great turnover number on a business earning under its cost of capital is motion without progress; EVA converts the spread into money, and sales-to-capital shows how hard each invested dollar works.

Is the report itself trustworthy?

Ratios take the statements as given. The guide to Z-Score, F-Score and M-Score asks the prior question. The Altman Z-Score blends five statement ratios into a bankruptcy gauge (safe above 2.99, distress below 1.81): Building Products' components — working capital/assets 0.10, retained earnings/assets 0.35, EBIT/assets 0.11, market equity/liabilities 1.40, sales/assets 1.10 — give Z = 2.913, the grey zone. Four clean ratios, one amber flag: exactly why a second lens exists. The Piotroski F-Score grades fundamental direction across nine checks (Building Products meets 7 — strong; Retail Chain meets 2 — weak), and the Beneish M-Score screens the statements themselves for manipulation patterns, flagging above −1.78. Health, direction, honesty: three different questions, three cheap answers.

From health check to valuation

A pass is a licence to value, not a conclusion.
