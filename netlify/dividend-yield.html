A 5% yield is a question, not a bargain: pricing income without the trap

Dividend yield is the oldest valuation shortcut in income investing: divide the annual dividend by a yield the stock “should” trade at, and you have a fair price. QuantaVal's dividend yield masterclass states the logic plainly — a stable payer tends to revert to its long-run average yield, so a current yield above that average suggests the stock is cheap. The method is one division long. Everything difficult about it hides in the two words “stable payer.”

A 5% yield can be a bargain or a warning. The dividend yield method prices the difference — but only after you interrogate the dividend itself.

The formulas

Dividend yield = Annual Dividend ÷ Price

Fair value = Annual Dividend ÷ Target Yield

Payout ratio = Dividend per Share ÷ EPS (and, separately, ÷ FCF per Share)

The target yield is not a guess: use the stock's own long-run average yield, checked against peers in the same sector. A utility and a staples name do not share a normal yield, and neither is priced off the market average.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Take a hypothetical company, Example Telecom Co. — invented for teaching only, not a real company.

  • Annual dividend (trailing 12 months): $1.80 per share
  • Share price: $36.00 → current yield = 5.0%
  • Its own 10-year average yield: 4.2%
  • EPS: $2.60; free cash flow per share: $2.10

Fair value = $1.80 ÷ 4.2% = $42.86

The stock yields 5.0% against its own 4.2% history, so on the yield method it trades 19.0% below fair value. That is the headline. Now the interrogation the headline requires:

  • Earnings payout = $1.80 ÷ $2.60 = 69.2%. Under the ~75% line QuantaVal uses for most companies — acceptable, not generous.
  • FCF payout = $1.80 ÷ $2.10 = 85.7%. The cash cover is much thinner than the earnings cover. The dividend is paid in cash, not in EPS, so this is the ratio that decides safety; check it on the CapEx / FCF calculator before trusting any yield.
  • Sustainable growth = ROE × retention. With an 11% ROE and 30.8% of earnings retained, the business self-funds growth of about 3.4% a year. A dividend growing faster than that is borrowing from the balance sheet.

Half a point of target yield moves fair value by more than 10%. The method does not remove judgment — it concentrates all of it into a single input, which is why the peer group and the averaging window matter more than the arithmetic.

The trap: when the yield is high because the price already knows

Yield rises when price falls, and prices usually fall first and explain later. Suppose Example Telecom drops to $24.00. The trailing yield is now 7.5% — apparently cheaper than ever. But if the market is pricing a cut, the trailing dividend is the wrong numerator: cut the dividend by a third to $1.20 and fair value at the same 4.2% becomes $28.57 — the “bargain” was the market doing the yield method with a smaller dividend than yours. QuantaVal's FAQ is blunt: yields above 8% often signal an expected cut. A high yield is a question — why is this cheap? — and the payout ratios, the FCF cover and the balance sheet are where the answer lives. When dividend yield fails

  • Buyback returners. A company returning cash through repurchases shows a small yield and a large total return. Yield alone calls it expensive; the buyback program calculator prices the other half of the return.
  • Growers with small payouts. For a payer whose dividend is small but compounding, the yield method has almost nothing to divide. Growing payers belong on the dividend discount model, which discounts the future dividend stream instead of capitalising today's.
  • Yield-plus-growth names. Where both income and growth matter, PEGY adds the yield to the growth rate instead of choosing between them.
  • REITs. Payouts above 90% are structural, not reckless; judge REIT income on AFFO, not on the earnings payout ratio.

Cross-checks before you act

A yield valuation says the income is mispriced; it does not say the business is good. Set it beside a full DCF, ask the reverse DCF what growth the price implies, and hold the name against the Graham Number floor.
