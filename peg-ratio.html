What you pay for growth: the PEG ratio, priced properly

The plain P/E multiple has a blind spot: it treats a company growing earnings at 20% a year and one growing at 2% as if they deserved the same price per dollar of earnings. Peter Lynch's fix was to divide the P/E by the growth rate. The result — the PEG ratio — answers a cleaner question: how much am I paying for each point of growth? QuantaVal's own PEG masterclass uses Lynch's benchmarks: a PEG around 1.0 is fair, below 1.0 is cheap for the growth on offer, and above 2.0 means you are paying a large premium for every point.

A P/E of 20 can be cheap and a P/E of 10 can be expensive. The difference is growth — and the PEG ratio is the tool that prices it.

The formulas

PEG = (Price ÷ EPS) ÷ EPS growth rate (%)

Fair P/E = Target PEG × Growth

Fair value per share = Target PEG × Growth × EPS

Note that the growth rate enters in whole percent — 16, not 0.16. Get that wrong and every PEG you compute is off by a factor of 100.

Worked example (hypothetical)

Take a hypothetical company, Example Logistics Software Co. — invented for teaching only, not a real company.

  • Share price: $48.00
  • Earnings per share: $2.40 → P/E = 20.0x
  • Expected EPS growth: forward consensus says 16% a year; the last five years actually delivered 22%

Which growth rate do you use? QuantaVal's rule is to take the lower of the consensus forecast and the historical record — forecasts are optimistic by profession — and to cap growth at 25% so one exceptional stretch cannot dominate the answer. Here that means 16%.

PEG = 20.0 ÷ 16 = 1.25

You are paying $1.25 for each point of growth, a quarter above Lynch's fair line. Run it backwards to a price: at a target PEG of 1.0, the fair P/E is 1.0 × 16 = 16.0x, so fair value = 16.0 × $2.40 = $38.40. The $48.00 price sits 25% above that. Even at a more forgiving target PEG of 1.1 (fair P/E 17.6x), fair value is $42.24 — still below the price. On this multiple, the growth is good but the price is better.

If growth comes in at 12% instead of 16%, the PEG at today's price jumps to 1.67 and fair value falls to $28.80. If the company actually delivers 20%, the price is exactly fair. Nothing else in the model moves — which is precisely the danger.

Why the growth estimate is the dangerous input

Growth sits inside the multiplier, so an error in growth is an error in the final price, undiluted. QuantaVal's masterclass puts it bluntly: overstating growth by five points inflates value by about a third — in the example above, assuming 21% instead of 16% lifts fair value by 31.2%. Three habits keep the input honest:

  1. Use a multi-year rate, and the lower of forecast vs history. A single spectacular year is not a growth rate. The equity research terminal shows the multi-year EPS record next to the forecasts, so the gap between the two is visible before you choose.
  2. Check growth against what the business can self-fund. Earnings cannot compound faster than equity does forever; sustainable growth is anchored by ROE times the retention rate. A PEG built on 20% growth from a company with a 10% ROE is quietly assuming outside capital.
  3. Distrust a very low PEG. A PEG under 0.5 usually means the growth estimate is too high, not that the market is giving growth away. A stock at a P/E of 12 with claimed 20% growth shows a PEG of 0.60 — cheap-looking, and worth one hard question about the 20% before it is worth anything else.

When PEG fails

  • Slow growers. A utility on a P/E of 10 growing at 2% shows a PEG of 5.0 — which reads as wildly expensive and is nothing of the sort. Below roughly 5% growth, the ratio stops being informative.
  • Negative or volatile earnings. A negative PEG has no valuation meaning, and a cyclical's peak-year EPS makes any growth rate built on it fiction.
  • Dividend payers. PEG prices earnings growth and ignores the yield entirely. For a payer, use PEGY, which adds the dividend yield to the growth rate — and check the yield itself with the dividend yield and payout calculator.
  • As a standalone verdict. PEG says nothing about cash conversion, leverage or moat. A cheap PEG on a business whose earnings never become cash is a cheap ticket to a bad business; cross-check ROIC vs WACC before acting.
